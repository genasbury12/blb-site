01

The feeling we are after

Before any of the technical parts. If a photo has this feeling and is slightly imperfect, use it. If it is beautiful and does not, do not.

The core feeling Warm morning light through a real kitchen window. Somebody at a table who is tired but not defeated. A cup that has been half drunk. Nothing staged, nothing gleaming.
Lived in A crumb on the counter is fine. Kids art on the fridge is better than a clean fridge.
Quiet, not empty Calm. Not styled into sterility. Warmth beats minimalism every time here.
Handwriting, not screens Pen on paper. A worn notebook. Real numbers in her own handwriting. Almost never a laptop, and never a stock photo of a graph.
One touch of coral A rust jumper, a terracotta mug, a red kettle. Something in frame that ties to the site.
Hands do a lot Half the shots have no face at all. Hands around a mug, hands writing, hands folding.
Mid moment Caught halfway through something. Mid laugh, mid pour, mid sentence. Not posed and waiting.
The test Would your reader look at this and think, that is my kitchen. Not, I wish that were my kitchen. That difference is the entire brand.
Soft edges Blurred background, sharp face. Depth is what stops a phone photo looking like a phone photo.
02

Yes and no

Read this once before you start. It will save you reshooting.

Do

  • Shoot mid morning, roughly nine to eleven
  • Turn every light in the house off, window light only
  • Face the window, or have it just off to one side
  • Take ten of every shot, not one
  • Leave space around the edges of the frame
  • Wear something soft in cream, sage, tan, denim or rust
  • Let the kitchen look like people live there
  • Use portrait mode for anything with a face
  • Keep the phone at your own eye level, not above
  • Shoot on an overcast day if you can, it is the best light there is

Do not

  • Shoot at midday, the light goes hard and unkind
  • Mix ceiling lights with daylight, it turns skin orange
  • Stand with the window behind you, you become a silhouette
  • Wear black, stark white, neon, logos or tight stripes
  • Use a filter. None at all, we colour match afterwards
  • Tidy the room until it looks like a showroom
  • Fill the frame edge to edge, we crop these several ways
  • Send them by text message, it destroys the quality
  • Screenshot a photo instead of sending the original
  • Wait until all seventeen are done to send anything

Two outfits is enough for the whole thing

Wear

  • Soft knits, chambray, denim, an apron
  • Cream, oatmeal, soft white, warm tan
  • Sage green, rust, terracotta
  • The red fleece from the bridge photo is perfect
  • Hair and makeup as you normally wear it

Avoid

  • Black and stark white, both are harsh on camera
  • Neon anything
  • Large logos or slogans
  • Busy patterns and thin stripes, which flicker on screens
  • Anything you would not normally put on at home
Why this matters. Your reader is a tired mom at her own kitchen table. She needs to recognise you, not admire you. Looking slightly under styled is working in your favour here.
05

Gather these first

Almost all of it is already in your kitchen. Ten minutes of collecting saves an hour of stopping and starting.

Objects

  • Two mugs you actually like
  • A worn notebook or planner
  • A pen you would really use
  • Real coffee or tea, not an empty cup
  • A small plant or a few flowers
  • A folded tea towel
  • A calendar or wall planner
  • A reusable grocery bag
  • Real groceries, unstyled

Printed, and filled in by hand

  • The 5-Minute Money Reality Check
  • The One-Page Monthly Budget
  • The Bill Due Date Calendar
  • The Grocery Trip Cheat Sheet
  • The Debt Payoff Tracker, some squares coloured in
  • Two or three others, any of them
Print them and use them before you photograph them. Write a few real numbers in. Let the paper curl a bit. A blank pristine printout looks like a product shot from a stock library. A half finished one looks like somebody is actually doing this, which is the entire point.

If you only manage four

Shots 1, 2, 6 and 11

The laugh, the three quarter portrait, the hands writing, and the filled in worksheet. Those four cover the homepage hero, the About page, the main body image and the product shot, which is most of what a visitor actually sees. Everything else can follow whenever you get to it.

08

Sending them over

The technical part, briefly, and then how to get them to us without wrecking them.

  1. Shoot at the highest quality your phone offers. Do not shrink anything, do not screenshot, and do not send through anything that compresses.
  2. Send everything, not just your favourites. We often pick a different frame than people expect, and the outtakes are frequently the best ones.
  3. Make a folder in Google Drive called Brand Photos. Inside it, one folder per setup. Setup One, Setup Two and so on.
  4. Name nothing. Genuinely, do not spend time renaming files. The folder tells us which setup it is and that is enough.
  5. Share the folder link and tell us what you could not get. We will work around anything missing rather than chase you for it.
  6. Send setups one and two first, before doing the rest. Those seven cover the homepage and About. We start placing them while you shoot the others.
Not by text message. Messaging apps compress photographs badly and there is no way to undo it. Drive, Dropbox or WeTransfer all keep the full quality.

Send one test photo first

Before you do the whole afternoon, take one shot of anything and send it over. Two minutes of feedback on the light and the framing beats reshooting an entire setup, and it has saved every client we have ever said this to at least one afternoon.
