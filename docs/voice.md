# Brand Voice and Copy Rules

## Voice in one sentence

Warm, blunt, confident, strategic, and human. Sound like an experienced big sister who has made the expensive mistakes, understands the customer's frustration, and will tell her what to do next without dressing it up.

## The balance

- Warm, but not overly sweet.
- Direct, but not harsh.
- Confident, but not arrogant.
- Emotionally perceptive, but not melodramatic.
- Strategic, but not corporate.
- Encouraging, but never empty or motivational for the sake of it.
- Conversational, but still polished and credible.

## How to write

- Use plain, natural English.
- Be specific. Name the actual problem, action, and result.
- Use short, punchy paragraphs.
- Keep one main idea in each paragraph.
- Explain the order of operations. The reader should know what comes first and why.
- Use concrete examples and lived experience.
- Speak to what the reader is thinking but may not be saying.
- Challenge the belief that more content or more traffic automatically creates income.
- Make the commercial point clear. What is being sold, to whom, and how does the content support the sale?
- Use emotional truth without turning every paragraph into a dramatic monologue.
- Be helpful and action-focused. Give the reader a next step.

## What the voice sounds like

- "Your blog isn't your business. It's your marketing."
- "Pretty and profitable aren't opposites."
- "I'm not interested in making you dependent on me."
- "Two years of nothing. Four months of something. Same person, same hours, different order."
- "No pitch, just a plan."
- "So close, babe."

Use "babe" sparingly. The existing brand guide says once or twice per page or email at most. It should feel natural, not pasted into every paragraph.

## Emotional language to use

Reflect the actual friction:

- She's posting and still not making money.
- She has too many ideas and cannot decide what matters first.
- She is tired of creating content that does not convert.
- She does not know what to sell or how to connect the pieces.
- She wants a business, not another pile of unfinished content.
- She needs a plan that works around real life and limited time.
- She wants to feel clear about what to do next.

Do not flatten these into generic statements like "she wants to grow her online presence." Say what is happening and why it is frustrating.

## Persuasion principles

1. Name the problem before presenting the solution.
2. Identify the belief keeping the customer stuck.
3. Challenge it with a clear explanation or relevant lived example.
4. Show the practical alternative.
5. Explain what the offer helps her implement.
6. Address objections honestly.
7. Sell with certainty, not pressure.

Name objections before the reader has to raise them. Do not dismiss concerns about money, time, traffic, or previous failed programs. Explain what the offer does and does not do.

## Avoid

- Empty hype and generic inspiration.
- Corporate jargon and formal consultant language.
- Vague promises such as "transform your online presence" without explaining the mechanism.
- Cliches such as "embark on a journey" or "saturated market."
- Making traffic sound irrelevant. The point is that traffic needs a commercial purpose.
- Overpromising income, speed, or guaranteed outcomes.
- Shaming the customer for inconsistency or overwhelm.
- Calling the customer lazy or implying that her problem is a character flaw.
- Making the founder sound like a guru who has never struggled.
- Repeating "babe" or other pet names excessively.

## Formatting and punctuation

- Never use em dashes or en dashes. Use a period, comma, colon, or the word "and" instead.
- Avoid emojis unless Genasys specifically requests them.
- Prefer readable paragraphs, useful headings, and clear bullets.
- Do not over-format every sentence for emphasis.
- Use contractions where natural.

## Founder voice versus brand voice

The founder can use first-person stories, opinions, and specific details from her experience. The brand should turn those stories into a useful lesson for the customer.

Founder story examples that can support messaging:

- Starting with an old 2010 MacBook in her aunt's basement.
- Spending roughly two years following the traditional blogging path without the income she wanted.
- Changing the order by learning business, sales, copywriting, and design, then selling services and offers.
- Building Boss Lady Bloggers into a multi-six-figure brand.
- Learning that beautiful design and business strategy need to work together.
- Rebuilding the business around a more realistic workload and better systems.

Do not invent scenes, dialogue, customer stories, or exact revenue figures to make the story more dramatic.

## Calls to action

Use clear action language. Tell the reader what happens when she clicks or replies. Examples include:

- See how the program works.
- Build the offer behind your blog.
- Get the details.
- Book a call.
- Start building your sales path.

Avoid pressure tactics, fake scarcity, or a CTA that does not match the actual next step.

## Editing checklist

Before finalizing copy, ask:

- Does this sound like a real person talking to a smart woman?
- Have I named the actual problem rather than summarizing it in marketing language?
- Is the point specific and useful?
- Does the copy connect content to an offer and a sale?
- Is the promise honest and supported?
- Does the reader know what to do next?
- Have I removed corporate filler, generic hype, and unnecessary punctuation?
